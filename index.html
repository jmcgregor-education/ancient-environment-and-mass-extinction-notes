Part 1

How do we know what ancient climates were like?

Climate is the average weather of a place over a long time. Nobody was around with a thermometer 300 million years ago, so geologists rely on proxies: clues preserved in rock, fossils, sediment, or ice that stand in for measurements we can’t take directly.

A quick preview: greenhouse gases

You’ll study this in detail in Unit 7, but you need the basic idea now. Gases like carbon dioxide (CO₂) and methane are greenhouse gases: they let sunlight in but trap some of the heat Earth gives off. In general, more CO₂ in the atmosphere means a warmer planet, and less CO₂ means a cooler one. Volcanoes add CO₂; weathering of rock and burial of plant material remove it. Keep that balance in mind; it shows up all through this lesson.

Clue 1: The rocks themselves

Many rocks only form under certain conditions. Find one in the record, and you know something about the climate when it formed. Select each rock type below to see a drawing of what it looks like and what it tells you. Pay attention to the drawings: you’ll sketch some of these in your notebook.

Clue 2: Fossils

Living things are adapted to particular climates, so their fossils are climate clues too. Fossil palm trees in Wyoming show that the region was once subtropical, a bit like Florida today. Fossil pollen in lake mud records which plants grew nearby, and the mix of plants shifts as climate cools or warms. Even fossil leaves help: plants have tiny pores called stomata, and leaves grown in high-CO₂ air need fewer of them. Counting stomata on fossil leaves gives an estimate of how much CO₂ was in the ancient air.

Clue 3: Chemistry in shells and ice

Oxygen comes in a light form (oxygen-16) and a heavy form (oxygen-18), called isotopes. Water with the lighter oxygen evaporates more easily. During cold times, that evaporated water falls as snow and gets locked up in ice sheets, which leaves the ocean slightly richer in heavy oxygen-18.

Tiny shelled organisms called foraminifera build their shells from ocean water, so their shells record the ocean’s oxygen mix. When they die, their shells pile up on the seafloor. Drill a sediment core, measure the oxygen in the shells layer by layer, and you get a record of how much ice was on Earth.

For the more recent past, ice cores drilled from Antarctica and Greenland are even more detailed. Snow piles up in yearly layers and turns to ice, trapping tiny bubbles of air. Scientists crush the ice and measure the ancient air directly, including its CO₂. You’ll come back to ice cores in Part 4.

Check yourself
  1. A geologist finds a thick layer of coal with fossil ferns, and right above it, a layer of rock salt. How did the climate change?

    It went from warm and wet (swampy forests that became coal) to hot and dry (shallow water evaporating to leave salt). The younger layer is on top, so the drying came later.

  2. A sediment core shows foraminifera shells suddenly getting richer in oxygen-18. What does that suggest?

    Ice sheets were growing. Light oxygen-16 was being locked up in ice on land, leaving the ocean, and the shells built from it, richer in heavy oxygen-18. That points to a cooling climate.

Part 2

Earth’s big climate swings

Using these proxies, geologists have found that Earth’s climate has swung between two broad states. In icehouse times, there are permanent ice sheets at the poles (we’re in one now). In hothouse times, there is little or no ice anywhere, and sea levels are high. Select each event on the timeline to see what happened and how we know.

Ice makes more ice: albedo

Albedo is how much sunlight a surface reflects. Ice and snow reflect most of the sunlight that hits them, while dark ocean and land absorb it. So when ice spreads, Earth reflects more sunlight, cools further, and grows even more ice. That kind of self-reinforcing loop is how a cooling climate can tip into a deep freeze like Snowball Earth.

Tectonic events in the record

Climate swings often line up with big events in Earth’s crust. You’ll learn why plates move in Unit 4. For now, focus on what these events leave behind in the rock record:

  • Supercontinents. Around 300 to 200 million years ago, the continents were joined into one landmass, Pangaea. Matching rocks and fossils on continents that are now oceans apart record it. You’ll examine that evidence in Unit 4.
  • Flood basalts. At a few moments in history, enormous volumes of lava poured out over a short time, building stacks of basalt flows thousands of feet thick. These flood basalts released huge amounts of CO₂. Several line up exactly with mass extinctions.
  • Mountain building. Rising mountain ranges expose fresh rock to weathering, which slowly pulls CO₂ out of the air. Some cool periods line up with major mountain-building events.
  • Sea level changes. Rock layers show shallow seas flooding the continents and then draining away. When ice sheets grow, sea level drops; when they melt, it rises.
Texas connection

During the warm Cretaceous, a shallow sea covered much of Texas. The limestone and chalk under Central Texas, including the rock around the Glen Rose dinosaur tracks and the Austin Chalk, formed on that seafloor. Earlier, in the Permian, West Texas held the Capitan Reef. Later, the deep basin beside the reef dried out and filled with thick layers of gypsum and salt. Both tell climate stories you can still see in the rocks.

Check yourself
  1. Why was finding glacial deposits right next to tropical limestone such a puzzle for Snowball Earth researchers?

    Limestone usually forms in warm, shallow seas, and glacial deposits form in cold places with ice. Finding them together, in rocks that formed near the equator, suggested ice reached even the warmest parts of the planet.

  2. How do growing ice sheets affect sea level, and why does that matter for ocean life?

    Water locked up in ice on land comes from the ocean, so sea level drops. Shallow seas over the continents drain away, and most ocean life lives in those shallow waters, so habitat shrinks.

Part 3

Mass extinctions: reading the case files

Species go extinct all the time. That steady, ordinary rate is called background extinction. A mass extinction is different: a large share of Earth’s species disappears in a geologically short time, and it shows up in the rocks as a sharp drop in the number and variety of fossils at one level. Below the boundary, many kinds of fossils; above it, far fewer.

Remember from Unit 3.3 that geologists drew many era and period boundaries at exactly these levels. Five events stand out as the biggest, known as the Big Five. Select each one.

The pattern behind most of them

Look across the case files and a pattern appears: most mass extinctions line up with rapid climate change. The end-Ordovician was a fast cooling. The end-Permian and end-Triassic line up with flood basalt eruptions that pumped out CO₂ and warmed the planet quickly. Warm oceans hold less oxygen and circulate less, so anoxia (oxygen-starved water) spread, recorded in layers of black shale. The end-Cretaceous added a sudden shock from space on top of ongoing eruptions in India.

Speed matters. Life has adapted to slow changes many times. The extinctions happened when conditions changed faster than many species could cope with.

Case file: the end-Cretaceous impact

In 1980, a team led by geologist Walter Alvarez and his father, physicist Luis Alvarez, studied a thin clay layer at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary in Italy. It held far more of the element iridium than normal Earth rocks. Iridium is rare in Earth’s crust but common in asteroids, so they proposed that a giant asteroid had hit Earth. Geologists tested the idea and found more evidence around the world:

  • The same iridium anomaly in the boundary layer at sites across the globe
  • Shocked quartz, crystals deformed by an enormous shock wave
  • Tiny glass beads (spherules) of melted rock that rained back down
  • Tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico, including along the Brazos River in Texas
  • And finally, the crater itself: Chicxulub, about 180 km (110 miles) wide, buried under Mexico’s Yucatán Peninsula and the Gulf of Mexico, and the right age

Dust and gases from the impact likely blocked sunlight, collapsing food chains on land and in the sea. Roughly three-quarters of species disappeared, including all dinosaurs except birds.

After the dying: recovery

Mass extinctions empty out ecosystems. In the fossil record, the layers just above an extinction boundary are often dominated by a handful of hardy survivors, like the clam Claraia after the end-Permian or ferns right after the end-Cretaceous. Over millions of years, the survivors diversify and fill the empty roles. After the end-Permian, reptiles took over the land; after the end-Cretaceous, mammals did. Each extinction reset who ruled the next era, which is part of why the boundaries make such clear dividing lines on the time scale.

Check yourself
  1. What’s the difference between background extinction and a mass extinction?

    Background extinction is the normal, steady rate at which species die out. A mass extinction is when a large share of all species disappears in a geologically short time, showing up as a sharp drop in fossil diversity at one level in the rocks.

  2. Why was iridium in the boundary clay such an important clue?

    Iridium is rare in Earth’s crust but common in asteroids. A spike of it in a single worldwide layer pointed to material from a huge impact spreading across the planet at one moment.

  3. How did massive volcanic eruptions lead to the end-Permian extinction?

    The Siberian Traps released enormous amounts of CO₂, which warmed the planet rapidly. Warm oceans held less oxygen and stopped circulating well, so oxygen-starved water spread and much of marine life died.

Part 4

Connecting deep time to today

The last part of the story uses the most detailed records we have. Ice cores from Antarctica reach back about 800,000 years, through many ice ages.

What the ice cores show

Over the last 800,000 years, Earth has cycled between long, cold glacial periods and shorter, warm interglacials, roughly every 100,000 years. We live in an interglacial now, called the Holocene. The air bubbles show that CO₂ rose and fell along with temperature through every cycle, staying between about 170 and 300 parts per million (ppm). A part per million means that out of every million molecules of air, that many are CO₂.

Historical and measured records

Proxies get more precise as they get closer to the present, and eventually direct measurements take over:

  • Historical records: written accounts, ship logs, harvest dates, and the dates lakes froze and thawed, kept for centuries in some places
  • The instrument record: thermometer readings from weather stations since the 1800s, and later satellites
  • Direct CO₂ measurements: continuous readings of the air at Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii began in 1958

Those measurements show atmospheric CO₂ now above 420 ppm, compared with about 280 ppm before the 1800s. That’s higher than at any point in the 800,000-year ice core record.

A simplified sketch, not the actual data. It shows the general pattern from the Antarctic ice core record (teal) and the modern measured value (red). The real record has many more ups and downs.

Comparing then and now

Scientists compare the modern record to the past in two ways. One is size: how much CO₂ and temperature have changed. The other is the rate of change: how fast. In the ice core record, the swings between glacial and interglacial CO₂ levels typically took thousands of years. The modern rise from about 280 to over 420 ppm has happened in less than two centuries.

Comparing rates across such different timescales is tricky, because rock records rarely capture short events in fine detail. That’s one reason scientists combine many kinds of evidence, the same approach you’ve seen throughout this lesson.

  2. What do ice cores show about CO₂ and temperature over the last 800,000 years?

    They rose and fell together through each glacial cycle, with CO₂ staying between about 170 and 300 ppm.

Key vocabulary

  • Proxy: a preserved clue that stands in for a measurement
  • Greenhouse gas: a gas that traps heat, like CO₂
  • Tillite: rock made from glacial debris
  • Evaporite: salt or gypsum left when water evaporates
  • Isotopes: forms of an element with different masses
  • Albedo: how much sunlight a surface reflects
  • Flood basalt: a huge outpouring of lava
  • Mass extinction: a large share of species lost quickly
  • Anoxia: water with little or no oxygen
  • Iridium anomaly: a spike of iridium marking an impact
  • Interglacial: a warm stretch within an ice age
  • ppm: parts per million
